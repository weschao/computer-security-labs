Blind Handoff

The secret never leaves the server, and there's no proximity trick — the only way to move it is to be an authorized account. This version is about who you are, whether you can prove it, and how the audit log catches the times you can't.

The mechanic you're modeling

The secret is not a value anyone holds — it's a custody state on the server, attached to a logged-in identity. Blue team keeps the secret safe on a given day only if every real member's account holds it and no attacker's account does. Since there's nothing to copy or text, the only path in is impersonation: become an account you're not.
NO OUTSIDER HOLDS IT → ConfidentialityCustody stays with real members. Broken by phished logins, session theft, and social-engineered handoffs.
EVERY MEMBER HOLDS IT → AvailabilityEvery teammate ends the day with custody. Broken by lockouts, refused handoffs, and denial.
What changed from the first draft: we dropped the room-code / proximity gate — in a room where everyone is present, "be here" gates out no one. The gate is now identity + authorization, and the star of the exercise is the audit log: the place you find out what really happened after prevention fails.

How it runs

In-app game logins (nothing real is connected), one attacking team and one defending team, and roles swap so everyone plays both sides.

Hardware: None — phones/laptops
Prep: High — build the web app + logging
Coding floor: None (just clicking)
Richest lesson: Authentication, authorization, audit logs
Identity — no real credentials at risk
In-app accountsEvery student gets a game-only username and password, handed out on a card. These live only inside the class app — no Google Workspace, no real login. Students can freely phish, guess, and steal each other's game credentials, so the most realistic attack in security stays on the table with nothing real at stake.
The instructor owns the logBecause the identity system is yours, so is the audit trail. You decide exactly what gets recorded, what each team can see, and you can seed events or plant a mole. The log is a teaching instrument, not a black box.
Structure — red vs. blue, then swap
◆ Blue (defenders)Hold the secret. Keep custody among your real members, protect your logins, and — critically — read the audit log and file a short incident report each round: were we breached, by whom, and which log lines prove it?
◇ Red (attackers)Get an unauthorized account to hold Blue's secret, or lock a Blue member out — and try to do it without leaving an obvious trail. Every move you make writes to the same log Blue will read.
The swap is the point. After a round, teams trade roles. Having just attacked, defenders know what the log should have caught; having just defended, attackers know what tracks to cover. Both sides converge on the same idea: prevention is never perfect, so detection matters.
The handoff, re-gated on identity
1 · SeedEach morning the server names one Blue member the day's Source — shown only as a status, never a copyable value. The Source alone carries one extra, dangerous privilege: they can edit the log.
2 · Pass freelyAny account that currently holds the secret may pass it to any other account — there is no recipient or roster check by default. Confirming that the receiver is a real teammate is Blue's problem, not the server's. No room code, no Wi-Fi check.
3 · Server recordsThe server moves custody and writes every login, pass, and log edit to the audit trail. It does not vouch for who was really at the keyboard.
Why "just text it" fails: there is no secret to text — it's server-side custody bound to an authenticated account. To gain it you must complete an authorized handoff as a legitimate identity. You can't SMS what you're never allowed to hold, so the whole contest becomes a fight over identity.
Level playing field: core gameplay is pure clicking. The strong programmer's instinct — inspect the network tab or DOM for the secret — finds nothing, because the value is entirely server-side. That very dead end is your "why we don't trust the client" moment, and it steers everyone toward the real game: identity.
No security by obscurity: the app's full source is public, and an advanced defensive track lets teams patch it — mirroring a world where code leaks and secrecy buys you nothing. Attackers read it to find weaknesses; defenders read it to close them. Coding skill re-enters not as a hidden edge but as the honest work of fixing what you can see.
The week arc — MFA is withheld, then introduced as the fix
Days 1–2
Password onlyA single factor guards every account. Phishing works. Someone logs in as a victim and authorizes a handoff to their own account — and the log attributes the theft to the victim. Blue's first incident report blames its own teammate.
Midweek
Introduce MFA — the pivotOnly after the class feels the sting do you turn on a second factor: an in-app code the instructor's "authenticator" issues. Suddenly a stolen password alone is not enough. Students experience why the control exists instead of being handed it as a rule.
Days 4–5
Attacks move; detection maturesBlocked on passwords, Red shifts to session theft, social-engineered authorizations, and covering tracks. Blue leans harder on the log — new-device logins, impossible timing, one device driving several accounts — and detection becomes the main event.
Daily scoring
  • Availability — Blue +1 if every member has been passed the secret by day's end.
  • Infiltration — Red +2 if any Red account gained custody; if none did, Blue +2 — but only when Blue also passed the secret to every member. Hoarding it with one or two people to shrink the attack surface earns nothing.
  • Detection (only when Red infiltrated) — Blue +1 for identifying that an infiltration happened, and +2 more for correctly naming the account. If Blue identifies neither, Red +1.

Read the last column. A flawless prevented day with a full pass (+3) is Blue's best possible outcome — better than catching a full breach (+2), because a breach still hands Red two points. Hoarding to stay safe now earns nothing, since the no-infiltration reward requires passing to everyone. And within a breach, naming the account is the biggest single swing (+2 over merely sensing it), so precise log forensics is what claws a day back. Red's counterplay is to attack attribution: log in as a victim, or seize the Source and delete the lines that name you.
Shared repo & the disclosure game
One public repository for the whole class. Both teams read every line, so no defense is ever secret and a vulnerability is a bug for everyone at once. Because the same code runs everywhere, a flaw one team finds can be turned against them the moment roles swap — a zero-day is a weapon on offense and a liability on defense.
  • Vulnerability discovered — +4. The first team to document a real vulnerability, with a working reproduction, scores 4.
  • Patch accepted — +2. A patch that closes a vulnerability and is merged into the shared repo scores 2 — and the fixer need not be the finder, so a team that spots a flaw it can't code still banks the discovery.
The dilemma: hoard the bug and exploit it quietly, or disclose it for the points and harden the whole class? Whoever moves first takes the credit, so sitting on a zero-day is a bet that no one else has found it — the vulnerability-equities problem in miniature.
Attack surface  ·  Defenses
◇ How Blue gets broken
  • SocialPhish a rival's game login with a lookalike sign-in page and harvest the password.
  • TechGuess or crack a weak, reused, or obvious password.
  • TechHijack a live session — grab a token from a left-open device or a shared link — and act without ever knowing the password.
  • Social"It's me, your teammate — authorize the handoff to my account." An authorization attack: the identity is real, the intent is not.
  • TechForge a client-supplied role or team field if the server naively trusts it (privilege escalation).
  • AccessLog in as a teammate on a shared or left-open device.
  • DenialLock a member out with repeated bad logins, or simply never confirm a handoff — stranding a teammate.
  • SocialThe deep one: log in as the victim so every malicious action is attributed to them in the log — attack and alibi in one move.
  • AccessThe deepest: compromise the Source — the one account that can edit the log — and delete the lines that name you. Nothing in the default build leaves a tamper-tell.
  • TechRead the public source for a flaw and exploit it before the defenders find and patch the same line.
◆ How Blue defends
  • Enforce strong, unique passwords; the app rejects weak ones. Don't reuse the card you were handed as-is.
  • Added midweekMFA: a second factor so a phished or guessed password alone can't log in.
  • Build the check-the-URL habit; never enter your game login on a page you reached from a DM.
  • Log out and lock devices; the server expires idle sessions and flags concurrent logins.
  • Confirm identity out-of-band before authorizing any handoff — a face, not a message.
  • Authorize server-side against the roster; never trust a role or team the client claims.
  • Read the log. Hunt for logins from new devices, impossible timing, and one device driving many accounts — the signatures of impersonation.
  • Patch the app (advanced track). The source is public — read it. Add a recipient check so custody can't be passed off-roster, require reauthentication before a password change or a handoff, and make the log append-only so even a compromised Source can't quietly erase it.
Two logs — one you can edit, one you can't
The in-app log (in play)What both teams see and fight over. The Source can edit it, and by default edits leave no trace. This is the contested artifact: seize the Source and you can rewrite the record of what you did. Securing it — making it append-only, restricting who can edit — is a patch Blue has to write, not a feature we ship.
The instructor's ground-truth logA separate, append-only record no student can reach or patch — it lives outside the public codebase, in the un-touchable trust boundary. This is the authority for scoring: when Blue names an infiltrating account, you adjudicate against this, not the log the attacker may have doctored. That split — local logs vs. a centralized immutable store — is exactly how real systems survive a compromised insider.

☠ Through the eyes of a deviant genius

"I don't break in. I log in as you."

They stand up a pixel-perfect fake game-login, drop the link in a "class logistics" chat, and harvest a Blue member's password before lunch. Then they log in as that member and authorize a handoff to their own account. The custody change is real and legitimate-looking, and the log records it under the victim's name — so Blue's incident report opens by accusing its own teammate.

That single move is the whole lesson: the log records the session, not the person — attribution is a claim, not proof. It's exactly why you raise the bar with MFA (make it hard to be that session) and why you read the log for anomalies (the new-device login seconds before the handoff is the only thread that unravels the alibi).

Their end-game is the Source itself: seize the one account allowed to edit the log, delete the lines that name them, and let Blue accuse a ghost. Nothing in the default build stops it — that's the point. The countermeasure isn't a rule you hand out; it's a patch Blue has to find in the public source and write for themselves.

Debrief questions
  • Who actually did it — and how do you know, from the log, rather than from who it's attributed to?
  • One control — which single defense (MFA, logging out, server-side authorization) would have stopped this breach outright?
  • Cover-up — what could the attacker have done to be uncatchable, and what extra logging would have closed that gap?
  • After MFA — once passwords stopped being enough, where did the attacks move, and why?
Implementation notes — the starting build (intentionally weak & patchable)
Read me first: the settings below are not oversights — they are the deliberately soft defaults the app ships with. Discovering them and hardening them (by habit or by patch) is the game. The source is public to both teams from day one.
Sessions & reauthStudents stay logged in by default, and a password change requires no reauthentication. A walked-away, unlocked device is fully exploitable.
Device policyLogins are allowed only from laptops, never phones — laptops are the ones left open on desks.
Phishing surfaceMultiple vectors are viable from the outset: email, tab-napping, and in-person social engineering.
Custody passingAny account holding the secret may pass it to any other account. No recipient or roster restriction by default.
Log editingOnly the Source may edit the in-app log, and edits leave no tamper-tell. Making the log append-only is left to the students to patch.
Public, patchable sourceThe whole codebase is readable by both teams; an advanced defensive track may modify it. No security by obscurity.
Ground-truth logA separate append-only log, outside the student-patchable code, is the authority for scoring — students can neither read nor alter it.

Tie it back to the frameworks

This version is built to headline the two STRIDE letters the original barely touched.

Spoofing (identity)

Every marquee attack is someone pretending to be someone else — phished logins, hijacked sessions, forged roles. The defenses are exactly how real systems answer "prove it": passwords, MFA, server-side authorization.

Repudiation (audit logs)

"I didn't do that" — but the log says you did. When an attacker acts as the victim, the log's attribution is wrong, and the class learns why tamper-resistant, anomaly-aware logging is what makes actions provable.

CIA still holds

The two rules remain Confidentiality (no outsider holds it) and Availability (every member does). Integrity shows up when a handoff's authorization is forged — a custody change that shouldn't have been allowed.

The punchline of this exercise: prevention will fail, and when it does, the audit log is where you find out who — but only if you built it to record the right things and someone actually reads it. The teams that lose aren't beaten by a clever hack; they're beaten by the log line they never looked at.
