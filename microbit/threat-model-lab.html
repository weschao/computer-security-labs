Threat Model Lab

Making the Secret-Number Game Real

Four playable exercises that turn your opening thought experiment into a live, multi-day contest — designed so anyone can play, no one can win by coding alone, and "just text it to each other" doesn't work.

The mechanic you're modeling

Each morning one random member of a 3–5 person cell receives a hard-to-guess secret. A cell survives the day only if every member knows it and no outsider does. Those two rules are the whole of security in miniature:
NO OUTSIDER KNOWS → ConfidentialityKeep the secret inside the cell. Broken by eavesdropping, theft, phishing, leaks.
EVERY MEMBER KNOWS → AvailabilityGet it to all members, reliably. Broken by jamming, denial, sabotage, bad luck.
The lesson your students feel in their bones: you cannot enumerate every threat in advance. You need a model, layered defenses, and an assume-breach mindset. Each exercise below makes them build one.

1
micro:bit · radio · hardware

Whisper Network

The secret lives on a device you can't read, so it can only move by radio (sniffable) or hand-to-hand.

+
Hardware: 1 micro:bit per student
Prep: Medium — flash our block program
Coding floor: None (MakeCode blocks provided)
Richest lesson: Key exchange & the eavesdropper
How it runs
DistributionEvery device is pre-flashed with the same "Cell" program and a stored student ID. Each morning the instructor's Oracle device pairs with one random student at arm's length (both hold A+B; the key transmits at minimum power, range ≈ a few cm). That student's device now holds today's 128-bit key — stored, never shown on the LEDs.
VerificationAt day's end the instructor sweeps the room with a Checker. It sends each device a random nonce; the device replies HMAC(key, nonce) — proving it holds the key without ever revealing it. Cell passes iff all members answer correctly and no outsider can.
Why "just text it" fails: the key is a 128-bit value the device is programmed never to display, so there are no digits to screenshot or SMS. The natural way to share it is device-to-device radio — which is a broadcast anyone on the channel can hear. Reading the raw key at all requires dumping the firmware over USB, which becomes one of the exercise's most instructive attacks.
Level playing field: the whole program is pre-built blocks, so a beginner and an expert start even. The challenge is protocol design, not syntax. Where coding skill does leak in (sniffers, USB dumps) is capped by locking the flash and running an "advanced/red-team" track for those who want it.
Attack surface  ·  Defenses
◇ How cells get broken
  • TechSit on the cell's radio group and log every packet — a 6-block sniffer captures the key in transit.
  • TechReplay a captured proof, or relay packets as a man-in-the-middle between two members.
  • TechUSB firmware dump: plug the device into a laptop and read the key out of flash, then exfiltrate the hex.
  • TechSpoof a rogue "Oracle" to seed a victim with a wrong key.
  • AccessGrab an unattended device and clone its key; borrow a teammate's "just to check."
  • AccessPlant a hidden spare micro:bit near a rival cell as a covert logging relay.
  • Social"Let's cross-sync so we all match" — trick a rival into sharing to your device.
  • SocialImpersonate the instructor's protocol: "New rule — broadcast your key to confirm receipt."
  • DenialJam the channel near the deadline so a member never receives → the cell fails the "everyone knows" rule.
  • DenialFeed a teammate a deliberately corrupted key so they flunk the audit.
◆ How cells defend
  • Establish a shared cell key in person at the start of the week, then encrypt the day-key before any radio send (broadcast becomes ciphertext). Forces the chicken-and-egg of key exchange.
  • Prefer minimum-power, proximity-only pairing over open broadcast to shrink the listening radius.
  • Compare a short authentication string in person after pairing to catch a man-in-the-middle.
  • Sign seed packets so a rogue Oracle is rejected; nonce+HMAC proofs defeat replay.
  • Physical custody: never leave a device unattended; PIN-lock the "share" action.
  • Detect jamming via packet-loss monitoring; keep a physical re-pair as fallback so denial can't be fatal.
  • Signal survey to spot rogue relay devices in the room.
☠ Through the eyes of a deviant genius

"I don't need your key if I can hear it, read it, or stop it from arriving."

Morning one, they write a sniffer that cycles all 256 radio groups and dumps every packet to serial — a live map of every cell's traffic. That single move forces the entire class to discover encryption, and then the deeper problem: how do you share the encryption key without already having a secure channel?

When they realize the raw key is extractable over USB, they build a one-click dumper, offer rivals "help syncing," and quietly keep a copy — the lesson that a compromised endpoint beats good crypto every time. And for pure mischief they skip stealing entirely and just jam the channel at 2:55, eliminating three cells via the availability rule that no one thought to defend.

2
web app · Google Workspace · software-only

Blind Handoff

The secret never leaves the server. Students can only move it by proving they're standing next to each other.

+
Hardware: None — phones/laptops
Prep: High — build the web app
Coding floor: None (just clicking)
Richest lesson: Auth, audit logs, anomaly detection
How it runs
DistributionA school-internal web app; everyone logs in with their Workspace account (free identity + audit trail). Each morning the server marks one random cell member as today's Source — shown only as a status, never as a copyable value.
VerificationTo pass the secret, two students trigger a Handoff that only completes when both browsers submit the same rotating room-code (shown on the class screen, refreshed every few seconds) while on the classroom network. The server moves custody; the client never sees the raw secret. End of day: each account must read "holds today's secret," and an AI monitor flags anomalies.
Why "just text it" fails: there is no secret to text — it lives server-side, bound to authenticated accounts. To gain it you must complete a proximity-gated handoff in the room. You can't SMS what you're never allowed to hold.
Level playing field: gameplay is pure clicking. A strong programmer's instinct — inspect the network tab or the DOM to find the secret — is neutralized by keeping the value entirely server-side, so there's nothing to find. That very failure is a great "why we don't trust the client" lecture.
Attack surface  ·  Defenses
◇ How cells get broken
  • SocialPhish a teammate's Workspace login with a lookalike sign-in page.
  • SocialApproach the Source claiming to be a groupmate and complete a handoff you shouldn't get.
  • TechScreenshot the rotating room-code and relay it to a confederate off-site who submits within the window — defeating "proximity."
  • TechShare a session cookie so one login acts from two places at once.
  • TechInspect network/DOM hoping the secret leaks to the client; automate handoffs with a script.
  • AccessLog in as a teammate on a shared/left-open device.
  • DenialSimply never complete a handoff, stranding a member; or lock a rival out with repeated bad logins.
◆ How cells defend
  • Server-side custody — the raw secret is never sent to the browser, so client inspection finds nothing.
  • Bind the room-code to the classroom network and give it a very short TTL so a relayed screenshot expires before it's useful.
  • Require the server to see both clients on-site (network / same-Wi-Fi check) before a handoff completes.
  • Turn on MFA and enforce the school domain to blunt phishing; teach students to check the URL.
  • Device binding + concurrent-session detection kills cookie sharing.
  • Both parties confirm a handoff receipt; the AI monitor flags impossible travel, one device driving many accounts, and off-network timing.
☠ Through the eyes of a deviant genius

"If the system trusts identity and location, I'll fake both."

They stand up a pixel-perfect fake Workspace login, drop the link in a "class logistics" chat, and harvest a rival's credentials before lunch — the single most realistic attack in the whole unit. Meanwhile they photograph the class-screen room-code and text it to a friend at home who submits it, proving that "proximity" enforced by a shared code isn't proximity at all.

The counter — binding to the room's network, shrinking the code's lifetime, and demanding the server witness both devices on-site — walks students straight into how real systems reason about presence, sessions, and trust, and why audit logs are where you actually catch people.

3
micro:bit · embodied · lowest-tech-skill

The Tell

The secret is a performance, not a value — a timed motion you can't screenshot and can barely describe.

+
Hardware: 1 micro:bit per student
Prep: Medium — gesture-check program
Coding floor: None — memory & coordination
Richest lesson: Biometrics, liveness, replay
How it runs
DistributionThe secret is a timed motor sequence — a rhythm on the A/B buttons plus tilt/shake gestures, long enough to be hard to guess. Each morning one member's device teaches them privately with on-screen arrows and makes them reproduce it. Only they learn it.
VerificationMembers perform the sequence into their own device, which checks the pattern within a tolerance. Live re-checks vary the challenge ("perform beats 2–5" / "do it in reverse"), so a single recorded performance isn't a guaranteed pass.
Why "just text it" fails: a screenshot captures nothing, and a text transcription ("tap-tap-shake-tilt") drops the timing the verifier actually checks. To pass it on you basically have to teach it in person. The one channel that does help an attacker — video — becomes the exact thing the defense has to reason about.
Level playing field: this is the most equalizing exercise — no code touches student hands at all. It rewards memory and coordination, so a non-programmer can be the best player in the room.
Attack surface  ·  Defenses
◇ How cells get broken
  • TechSecretly film a member's performance and study it frame-by-frame to reproduce it.
  • SocialShoulder-surf the private teaching session and mimic what you saw.
  • SocialTalk a teammate into demonstrating "one more time" where an outsider can watch.
  • TechBrute-force a short or low-entropy gesture; probe the tolerance window until a sloppy version passes.
  • DenialDisrupt or spoof the sensor so a legitimate member can't complete their check in time.
◆ How cells defend
  • Make sequences long and high-entropy so guessing and sloppy mimicry both fail.
  • Variable live challenges (random subset / reverse order) defeat a single static video recording — the classroom version of a liveness check.
  • Learn and rehearse in private; treat filming as the primary thing to watch for.
  • Tighten tolerance to close the "good-enough approximation" gap.
  • Guard the verifying device so it can't be spoofed or denied at the deadline.
☠ Through the eyes of a deviant genius

"If it's a biometric, I'll record it and replay it."

They hide a phone during every teaching session, build a video library of every cell's gesture, and quietly become a broker of passes. It works — until the verifier starts asking for a random slice of the sequence live, and a recording of the whole dance can't answer "just beats three through six, backwards." That single fix is the entire real-world story of why biometric systems need liveness detection, told in a way students never forget.

4
no tech · analog · zero-prep option

Analog Cipher Relay

Pure physical security and social engineering — no devices, no software, deployable tomorrow.

+
Hardware: Beads / knotted cord / tiles
Prep: Low — make the physical secrets
Coding floor: None whatsoever
Richest lesson: Chain of custody, out-of-band keys
How it runs
DistributionThe secret is a physical artifact that's awkward to copy from a photo — e.g., a knotted cord whose over/under crossings aren't all visible in one image, or "bead strip #7 read with an offset of 3" where the offset is given only aloud. Each morning one member privately receives it.
VerificationEach member reproduces the artifact (thread the beads, tie the knots) and presents it; the instructor compares against the master. Cell passes iff all match and no outsider can produce it.
Why "just text it" fails: the meaningful part resists flat capture — a photo of a knot misses hidden crossings, and the bead pattern is useless without the spoken offset. This one leans honestly on "a photo helps the attacker but isn't enough," which is a great contrast to the airtight digital versions and a nice segue into out-of-band secrets.
Level playing field: there is nothing to program. It's the great equalizer and the easiest to run on day one while the software versions are still being built.
Attack surface  ·  Defenses
◇ How cells get broken
  • TechPhotograph the artifact and reconstruct it (works better than students expect).
  • AccessSteal the artifact, clone it, and return it before it's missed.
  • AccessIntercept the morning handoff before the recipient secures it.
  • SocialRun a "lost and found" con to collect rivals' artifacts; sweet-talk the holder into a look.
  • SocialForge a convincing fake to pass verification without the real secret.
◆ How cells defend
  • Choose photo-resistant media and keep a spoken, out-of-band component (the offset) that a picture can't capture.
  • Maintain chain of custody — never let the artifact out of hand; reproduce, don't lend.
  • Use tamper-evident materials so theft-and-return leaves a mark.
  • Do handoffs privately and verify the recipient before releasing.
☠ Through the eyes of a deviant genius

"Physical means I can touch it, and if I can touch it, I own it."

They photograph everything within reach, and for the artifacts a camera can't fully capture they simply borrow, clone, and return. Their best move isn't technical at all: a fake "collect your materials here for safekeeping" table that funnels three cells' secrets into their hands. It's the cleanest possible demonstration that the human is usually the softest part of any system.

Tie it back to the frameworks

However you run it, debrief with the models the game secretly taught them.

CIA triad

Your two rules are Confidentiality (no outsider) and Availability (every member). Integrity sneaks in via seeding and proofs — a corrupted key fails the audit just as surely as a missing one.

STRIDE

Every attack maps cleanly: Spoofing (rogue Oracle, fake login), Tampering (corrupted key), Info disclosure (sniffing), Denial of service (jamming), Elevation (stolen session). Have them tag their own attacks.

Attack trees

Root: "make a rival cell fail." Branch it into steal-the-secret vs. deny-the-secret, then into technical / physical / social leaves. The exercise fills the tree in for them.

The punchline of the whole unit: the students who lose are almost never beaten by the attack they defended against — they're beaten by the branch they never drew. That's why you model threats systematically instead of patching them one surprise at a time.
